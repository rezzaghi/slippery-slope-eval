# How to Detect the Slippery Slope Fallacy

A slippery slope argument says that if we take one step, a chain of further steps will follow and end somewhere bad. Such an argument is not always a fallacy, because sometimes one step does lead to the next. It becomes a fallacy when the chain is assumed instead of shown.

The philosopher Douglas Walton describes four types of slippery slope argument. Each type links the first step to the bad outcome in a different way, so each is detected differently.

---

## 1. The Causal Slippery Slope Argument

**What it claims:** one event will *cause* the next, in a chain that ends in disaster.

**Example:**
> "If we let employees work from home on Fridays, soon they'll want Mondays too. Then nobody will come to the office, team culture will collapse, and the company will fail."

**How to detect it:**

Start by writing out the chain step by step: Fridays at home, then Mondays at home, then nobody in the office, then team culture collapses, then the company fails. That makes four links, and each one needs support.

Now check each link. Will working from home on Fridays lead to requests for Mondays? Possibly, since some employees may ask. Will a second day at home mean nobody comes to the office? No reason is given, and people would still be in the office three days a week. Will an empty office ruin team culture, and will that sink the company? Many companies work fully remote, so neither step follows automatically.

Then estimate the whole chain. The chance of reaching the end is roughly the chances of each link multiplied together. Even if each of the four links were 80% likely, the chance would be 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8, about 41%. These links are much weaker than 80%, so the real chance is far lower.

Next, ask what would push things from one step to the next. The argument names nothing: no rule, pressure or incentive that forces the company to allow Mondays once it allows Fridays.

Finally, look for places where the chain could stop. Management sets the office policy, so it can allow Fridays and refuse Mondays. A chain that someone can stop at any point is not a real slope, and this argument fails because none of its links is supported.

---

## 2. The Precedent Slippery Slope Argument

**What it claims:** if we treat *this* case a certain way, fairness will force us to treat *similar* cases the same way, and those cases get worse and worse.

**Example:**
> "If we give this student a deadline extension because a family member died, we'll have to give extensions to anyone with any excuse: a cold, a busy week, a party."

**How to detect it:**

Start with the reason for the first decision. The student gets an extension because of a death in the family, a serious event they could not foresee or control.

Then compare the later cases with that reason. A busy week can be foreseen. A party is a choice. A cold may or may not be serious. None of these shares the feature that justified the first extension, so granting it does not commit the teacher to granting the others.

Finally, test whether a rule can separate the cases. "Extensions for documented emergencies" includes the death in the family and excludes the party. Because such a rule is easy to write, the precedent has a clear limit, and the slope does not hold.

---

## 3. The Sorites Slippery Slope Argument

**What it claims:** there is no clear place to draw the line, so once you move it a little you have no principled reason not to move it all the way.

The name comes from the ancient *sorites* (heap) paradox: removing one grain from a heap of sand still leaves a heap, so when does it stop being one?

**Example:**
> Someone proposes reducing the legal workweek from 44 to 40 hours. The reply: "If you can cut 4 hours, why not cut to 1 hour? Why not 0?"

Note that this argument doesn't predict that 40 hours *will cause* 0 hours. It claims that accepting 40 *logically commits* you to 0.

**How to detect it:**

Start with the principle behind the proposal. The argument only works if the case for 40 hours is that fewer hours are always better. If that were the principle, 1 hour and 0 hours would follow. But the case for 40 hours is about balance: workers' health and free time against output, wages and costs. A balance has a best point somewhere, so supporting 40 does not mean supporting 0.

Next, notice that a fuzzy line is still a line. Nobody can prove that 40 hours is better than 39 or 41, but that does not make 40 and 1 the same. Voting ages work the same way: choosing 16 or 18 is partly arbitrary, yet nobody concludes there should be no age limit at all.

Then reverse the argument. If cutting hours is absurd because there is no stopping point, is raising them to 80 fine? The opponent will say no. That means they accept that a sensible range exists, and the real debate is where in that range the line should be: 44 or 40.

Finally, check what has happened before. Brazil cut the workweek from 48 to 44 hours in 1988, the ILO set a 40-hour standard in 1935, and France moved to 35 hours in 2000. None of these cuts led to further cuts toward zero.

---

## 4. The Full Slippery Slope Argument

**What it claims:** a combination of the above. A precedent, a vague line and a causal push work together to drive things down the slope.

**Example:**
> "Today the city charges a small fee for downtown parking. That sets a precedent for charging (*precedent*). Since 'a reasonable fee' is vague, each increase will seem small (*sorites*). And once the city depends on the revenue, it will keep raising prices (*causal*). Soon nobody will be able to afford to park downtown."

**How to detect it:**

Start by splitting the argument into its parts. It has three: a precedent (charging once means charging is accepted), a sorites (each increase is small, so there is no clear point at which to object) and a causal claim (the city will come to depend on the revenue and keep raising prices).

Then test each part as in the sections above. The precedent part: charging a small fee does not commit the city to any particular price, just as having a bus fare does not commit it to an unaffordable bus fare. The sorites part: the city council votes on each increase, and a fee that has doubled is easy to notice and oppose, so the line does not disappear. The causal part: this is the only claim with some force, since cities can come to rely on a source of revenue. But it needs evidence, such as how this city has handled other fees, and the argument gives none.

Notice that the whole argument rests on the causal claim. Without the city depending on the revenue, nothing pushes the fee upward, and the precedent and sorites parts have nothing to work with. That is the claim to check first.

Finally, don't be persuaded by the length of the argument. Three weak parts do not add up to one strong argument, and the chain still breaks at its weakest link.
